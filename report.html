7. Interpretation: How the Narrator's Language Connects Time, Memory and the Meaning of Human Life

7.1 Research Paradigm and What the Figures Can and Cannot Show

This study follows a reader-oriented approach to literary analysis. It examines how the narrator uses language, descriptions, symbols and emotional expressions to communicate ideas about time, memory and human existence. The meaning of a literary text is not understood only through its plot but also through the reader's experience and interpretation of the language.

My initial reading of the story about Alistair Vance, Evelyn and the Anamnesis machine helped me identify four major research dimensions: time and human existence, memory and emotional experience, loss and emotional duality, and meaning and value of life. These dimensions emerged from my reading of the story and became the foundation for the research question and computational analysis.

The computational approach follows the idea of algorithmic criticism (Ramsay, 2011), in which digital methods assist literary interpretation by identifying patterns that may not be immediately visible during ordinary reading. The purpose of the computational analysis is not to replace human interpretation but to support it with organised textual evidence.

The analysis identifies recurring words, expressions and phrases associated with the four dimensions. The figures show how these expressions are distributed across different segments and stages of the story. However, frequency alone cannot explain the complete meaning of a literary expression. A word may have different meanings depending on its context, and the emotional significance of a passage cannot always be measured through numerical counts.

Therefore, the numerical findings are used alongside close reading to understand how the narrator develops the story's central ideas.

7.3 Dimension 1: Time and Human Existence, or Beyond Mechanical Time

The first dimension explores how the narrator presents time through the symbolic language of clocks, mechanisms and mechanical movements. At the beginning of the story, the valley of Oakhaven is described as a place where time does not simply flow but appears to collect or pool. This unusual description immediately establishes time as something more complex than an ordinary measurement.

Alistair's workshop strengthens this idea. The clocks, ticking sounds, pendulums and mechanical movements create an atmosphere in which time appears organised and controlled. His work as a clockmaker reflects his belief in precision and the importance of understanding mechanical systems. The workshop can therefore be interpreted as a symbolic representation of his initial understanding of existence: life appears to follow a measurable rhythm.

However, the arrival of Evelyn and the Anamnesis machine gradually challenges this understanding. The machine introduces an experience in which time becomes connected with memory and emotion rather than simply mechanical movement. Alistair discovers that the past can remain emotionally present, even when the original events have ended.

This development changes the symbolic meaning of time in the story. It is no longer only something measured by clocks. It also becomes a way of understanding memory, personal experience and the passage of human life.

The ending makes this transformation especially meaningful. The statement, "Time was no longer a cage or a river carrying him away; it was a song they were all singing together," presents time as something shared and experienced rather than something that controls or separates people.

Thus, the story moves from a mechanical understanding of time towards a more emotional and human understanding of existence.

7.4 Dimension 2: Memory and Emotional Experience, or the Connection Between Past and Present

The second dimension examines the relationship between memory, emotions and personal identity. The Anamnesis machine is the central symbol through which the narrator develops this theme.

Initially, the machine appears to be an unusual mechanical object that requires Alistair's technical understanding. However, as he investigates it, he discovers that its operation is connected with the residual emotional energy left behind by the operator's life. This discovery introduces the idea that human memories are not simply records of past events. They also carry emotional experiences.

Alistair's experience of Clara becomes an important moment in the narrative. The memory is described as vivid and powerful, suggesting that the emotional effect of the past can remain alive in the present. His reaction shows that memory is connected not only with what a person remembers but also with what that memory makes them feel.

Evelyn's explanation of the machine further develops this idea. Her statement, "I brought it to you not to be cured of my past, but to see if the machine could be made whole," suggests that the purpose of memory is not necessarily to remove painful experiences. Instead, it may involve understanding and accepting them.

The story therefore presents memory as an important part of human identity. Past experiences, including painful ones, contribute to the way individuals understand themselves and their relationships with others.

Through the Anamnesis machine, the narrator connects mechanical technology with emotional experience and raises a larger question about whether human memories can be separated from the emotions that give them meaning.

7.5 Dimension 3: Loss and Emotional Duality, or the Relationship Between Sorrow and Happiness

The third dimension focuses on the emotional contrast between loss and happiness. The story suggests that human experience cannot be understood through positive emotions alone. Pain, regret, sadness and emotional loss are also important parts of life.

Alistair's encounter with memories from his past demonstrates how emotional experiences can remain unresolved. The appearance of Clara brings the past into the present and makes him confront feelings that he may have previously avoided. This suggests that memories can contain both emotional attachment and pain.

The Anamnesis machine also provides a connection between Evelyn's experiences and Alistair's own emotional understanding. Their interaction suggests that the purpose of remembering is not always to escape suffering but to recognise the importance of experiences that have shaped a person's life.

The story gradually moves beyond the opposition between happiness and sadness. Rather than presenting sorrow as something that must simply be removed, it suggests that difficult experiences can contribute to emotional understanding.

The contrast between fear and courage is particularly important. The story's statement, "Every choice, whether born of fear or courage," suggests that different emotional responses form part of the human experience. A person's life cannot be judged only by the choices that appear perfect or successful.

Therefore, loss and happiness are presented as connected parts of human existence. The story encourages the reader to understand emotional experiences as contributing to the completeness of life rather than treating painful memories as meaningless.

7.6 Dimension 4: Meaning and Value of Life, or the Completeness of Human Experience

The fourth dimension brings together the major ideas developed throughout the story. It examines how the narrator questions the meaning and value of human life.

At the beginning, Alistair's understanding of the world is closely connected with mechanical precision. As a clockmaker, he works with objects that must function accurately and maintain their proper rhythm. This mechanical environment can be interpreted as a symbol of his initial preference for order and control.

However, his experience with the Anamnesis machine gradually changes his understanding. Through memory and emotional discovery, he begins to recognise that human life cannot be measured by perfection alone.

The statement, "the value of a life was not measured by the perfection of its path, but by the completeness of its experience," expresses the central philosophical message of the narrative. It suggests that mistakes, difficult decisions, memories and emotional experiences are all important parts of an individual's life.

The reference to fear and courage further strengthens this idea. Every choice contributes to the development of a person's identity, even when the outcome is uncertain or painful.

The description of the "unique, beautiful architecture of the soul" presents human identity as something formed through different experiences rather than through an ideal or perfect life. The story therefore challenges the idea that a meaningful life must be free from suffering or mistakes.

The final interaction between Alistair and Evelyn provides an emotional conclusion to this development. Evelyn's statement, "You have given it back its heart," symbolically connects the restoration of the machine with the restoration of emotional meaning.

The Anamnesis is therefore more than a mechanical object. It represents the connection between memory, emotion and human experience. Through this symbol, the story suggests that the value of life lies in accepting and understanding the experiences that make each individual unique.

7.10 Limitations

This study has several limitations.

First, the analysis depends on selected words and phrases. Expressions such as "time", "memory", "heart" and "experience" can carry different meanings depending on their context. A numerical method may identify their occurrence without fully understanding their symbolic or emotional significance.

Second, the extraction was assisted by a language model. Although the extracted phrases were checked against the story, the selection of phrases remains dependent on the research prompt and the model's interpretation. Another model or reader might identify different expressions.

Third, the story was divided into seventeen segments and four narrative stages for analysis. This segmentation was based on changes in scenes and narrative development. A different division could produce different stage-level densities.

Fourth, short segments may produce high phrase densities because even a small number of occurrences can create a comparatively large rate per 100 words. Therefore, high density should always be considered alongside the actual number of occurrences and the context of the passage.

Fifth, the four research dimensions are connected and may overlap in literary meaning. For example, a reference to memory may also communicate emotional loss or contribute to the meaning of life. Assigning each extracted phrase to one sub-dimension helps avoid double-counting, but it cannot represent every possible interpretation.

Finally, numerical counts show the distribution of selected vocabulary rather than the complete meaning, tone or emotional depth of the story. Close reading remains necessary for interpreting the narrator's language and the characters' experiences.

7.11 Conclusion

The story of Alistair Vance, Evelyn and the Anamnesis machine explores the relationship between time, memory, emotions and the meaning of human life. Through the four research dimensions, the analysis demonstrates how the narrator gradually moves from a mechanical understanding of time towards a more emotional and philosophical understanding of existence.

At the beginning, clocks and mechanical movements establish time as something that can be measured and controlled. The arrival of the Anamnesis machine challenges this understanding by connecting time with memories and emotional experiences. Alistair's encounter with the past shows that memories remain important because of the feelings and experiences they carry.

The story also presents sorrow, happiness, fear and courage as connected parts of human life. Rather than suggesting that a meaningful life must be perfect, it argues that different experiences contribute to the development of individual identity.

The final understanding that the value of life lies in the completeness of experience brings together the major themes of the narrative. The Anamnesis machine becomes a symbol of the relationship between memory and human emotion, while its restored heart represents the recovery of meaning and connection.

Ultimately, the story suggests that time should not be understood only through clocks, nor should human life be judged only through perfection. A meaningful life is formed through memories, choices, emotional experiences and the acceptance of both joy and sorrow. The story therefore presents human existence as a shared and continuing experience rather than a mechanical process that can be perfectly controlled.
